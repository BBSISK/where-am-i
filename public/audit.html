history		The spa that tasted of eggs
In 1758, a sulphur spa was discovered on the estate of Agmondisham Vesey – a name that deserves a story of its own – and Lucan became a health resort. The well was almost 7 feet long and 2 feet wide, right on the brink of the Liffey, so floods kept getting into it until Mr Vesey built a wall. The claims were impressive: skin complaints, rheumatism, paralysed limbs, itch and ringworm. One man with loose teeth and sore gums washed his mouth with the water, drank some, and declared himself completely cured. The taste was another matter. One writer, Rutty, said it tasted of a boiled egg – and when strongest, of a putrid one – and smelt of sulphur. Still, the spa stayed popular until about 1845, when fashion moved on. Spas weren't quite finished in Lucan, though: the Spa Hotel was built in 1891 to a design by W.M. Mitchell, with an annexe for spa treatment rooms.

history		Three bridges and a cheeky poem
Lucan has had three bridges over the Liffey, and each one has a tale. The medieval bridge stood near Lucan House. In 1456 a statute ordered a tower with a gate on the bridge, to keep 'Irish enemies and English rebels' out of Fingal by night. Only a single pointed arch of it survives. Then came an 18th-century bridge. The old road by the river was so dangerous that several people had lost their lives and many cattle had drowned, and in 1731 an act allowed a new bridge. Dean Swift wrote a couplet about it: 'Agmondisham Vesey out of great bounty, built the bridge at the expense of the county.' Ouch. That bridge was swept away in the floods of 1784 or 1786. The bridge there today was built in 1814: a single arch of about 110 feet. It's been described as the longest span masonry arch ever built in Ireland – and back in 1859, as far more expensive than the occasion called for. It cost over £9,000. Worth every penny.

history		Pass the manor: the Sarsfields and Lucan House
Lucan's history is a long game of pass-the-manor. Around 1185 it went to Alard FitzWilliam, and by 1204 to the Peche family, who held it for much of the 13th century. In the later middle ages it belonged to the FitzGerald earls of Kildare, who turn up everywhere around here. After their fall, following the Silken Thomas rebellion, Sir William Sarsfield bought it, and the Sarsfields kept it until the middle of the 17th century. Cromwell dispossessed them, and a Parliamentarian colonel, Theophilus Jones, took over. But the Sarsfields were restored just in time for Patrick Sarsfield to be made Earl of Lucan. Lucan House was built around 1775, partly designed by William Chambers, with plasterwork by Michael Stapleton, and the medieval castle is thought to have stood beside it. Today it's the residence of the Italian Ambassador. Quite a step up from pass-the-manor.

Beside Lucan House is the ruin of St Mary's, the old parish church, and it hides something very special in its north wall: pottery jars, set into small round holes. smr:KD011-004002-: Castle - Anglo-Norman masonry castle. LEIXLIP. Just S of the confluence of the River Liffey and its tributary the Rye Water. According to Bradley et al. (1986 vol. 3, 309-311), Adam de Hereford's grants to St. Thomas' Abbey, which mention his castle of 'Hernie' or 'Ernia', can be dated to before 1212. While the original castle may have been a motte, by the time of the grants the castle had a grange and a chapel attached. Around 1252, the manor of Leixlip passed from Adam's son, Stephen, to the Pippard family. In 1302, Ralph Pippard granted all his manors in Ireland to the King, and in 1303-4, monies were spent on constructing a kitchen in the castle, building a limekiln to produce lime for works on the castle, breaking stone and cutting wood. architecture		St Patrick's College
The idea of a college in Maynooth goes back to 1518, when the ninth Earl of Kildare asked for a licence to found and endow one. It took a while to happen. excavation		What lay beneath the keep
Castles have a habit of sitting on top of older secrets, and Maynooth's is no exception. In 1996, archaeologists excavated the entire inside of the keep and found seven main phases of life stacked on one spot – a sort of historical lasagne. At the bottom: part of a prehistoric building, plus a stone axe head and an unfinished macehead. Somebody never got around to finishing that job. Above that, at least two small round houses of post and wattle, probably early medieval, one with a curving wooden stockade. Then the land was farmed, and the furrows ran right over the old houses. The site fell into Norman hands around 1175, and from then comes a mound of sod about a metre high, with a wooden building on top and a stout fence around it. Left behind: an arrowhead, an iron spur and a scabbard chape. Only after all that, probably in the late 1180s, did the stone keep start to rise.

Before motorways, there were canals. This stretch of the Royal Canal was built between 1790 and 1796 to link the east of Ireland with the west, and for some years it helped make Maynooth prosperous. smr:KD005-009002-: Church. LARAGHBRYAN EAST. The church is mentioned in Strongbow's confirmation of the Glendalough possessions as belonging to Glendalough in 1173, and was later subservient to St. Mary's Maynooth (KD005-016----) with which it was united in 1518. According to the OSL (Herity 2002, 15-16) Archdall records the death of the First Earl of Kildare at Laraghbryan in AD 1316. It was in good condition in 1615, and in 1630, although by the latter date the chancel was 'uncovered' (IHR 1941a, 47-8; IHS, 98). Stands to SW of centre of a graveyard (KD005-009003-). A fairly well-preserved, but completely ivy-clad, rectangular structure (int. dims. L 24.9m E-W; Wth 5.9m) built of coursed, limestone blocks (walls av. T 0.8m) with roughly dressed quoins, comprises a nave (int. dims. L 14m; Wth 5.9m) and slightly narrower chancel (int. dims. L 10.9m; Wth 5.35m), with a later, three-storied, rectangular tower (int. dims. L 3.5m E-W ; Wth 2.5m: wall T 0.6m) abutting the W gable wall. The nave can be entered through either of two, almost opposing, opes near the W ends of the N (Wth 3.5m) and S walls (Wth 3.3m), which are probably enlarged original doorways. The nave is lit by one window in the N wall and two in the S wall; all tall, pointed-arched and set in broadly splaying, round-arched embrasures and probably 14thC in date, and plaster survives on the wall faces. The slightly narrower chancel has a modern burial vault inserted into its E end where the original E window is robbed-out. It is lit by one window in the N wall and three in the S, one of which is now blocked-up, all similar to those in the nave. There are two doorways in the N wall. The more easterly one (Wth 1.5m) appears to be a later insertion into an older window ope opposite a similar ope in the S wall, while the more westerly one (Wth 1.8m) is almost opposite a third doorway (Wth 1.3m) which also appears to be a later insertion into an older window ope into which a single light, cusped ogee-headed window head with hood-moulding, and concave jambs (15thC?) was previously inserted, in the S wall. The later tower is entered from the nave through a low, narrow (Wth 0.87m) round-headed doorway. The ground-floor is lit by a loop in the SW angle from where a lintelled intramural stairs leads to first-floor level which is lit by a loop in the N and S walls and contains a wall-cupboard in the N wall. A spiral stairs in the NW angle leads to second-floor level which is lit by a loop in the SW angle. Compiled by: Gearóid Conroy Date of upload: 10 June 2011
